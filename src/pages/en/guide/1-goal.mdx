The goal you hand a bot works best as one sentence: "I want …, so that …". The first half is easy. The hard part is the second half, because how far should "so that" go? In [the article](https://x.com/poteto/status/2123159537016533493), poteto's answer is to keep going until you reach what the work does for the business.

<mark><strong>Keep asking "what is this for?" until you reach whether it earns you money or saves you money. That's your goal.</strong></mark>

## Take bookings, and ask twice

Say you run a small business and customers book ahead. Each time a new customer gets in touch, you read the email, check your schedule, and send a confirmation. Do that long enough and the job turns into "send confirmation emails" in your head. Hand it to a bot that way, and it will send emails and nothing more.

That's a mistake the author sees often, treating the process as the goal. Filling out a form or sending an email is one step in the process. So ask one level up. What's the email for? So every new customer's booking is scheduled. Ask once more. What does a scheduled booking do for the business? A customer with a booking actually shows up, so it earns you money.

The other side is saving money, and the author's example is stock. Say you fill in a stock sheet every week. Ask what it's for, and the answer is ordering just the right amount, not too much and not too little, so it saves you money. For earning money, the author's examples are closing a sale and building new features.

If you can't work it out yourself, that's normal. Ask your manager or a colleague, or just ask the bot.

## Once you know that, the process can change

Why dig this deep? Because a bot that knows where the finish line is can help find the route. You may already have a well-worn process at work, but it's only one way to reach the goal. If the bot knows you want "every new customer's booking scheduled," not just "emails sent," it has room to find something simpler than what you do now. That's why the author warns against getting too attached to your current process. It's a means to an end.

It's also why this step covers the goal and not the process. The process comes in step 2. If you'd rather the bot follow your process exactly, you can say so then.

## One goal, one bot

Once the goal is clear, give it a bot of its own. Make a new <Term k="bot" /> as in [beginner lesson 2](/en/start/2-first-bot/). Any name will do. If nothing comes to mind, call it Potato and let it pick a name in a minute.

Why a new one? The official docs say a bot with one job builds more useful background than a catch-all helper. A bot that only handles bookings keeps what it learns about bookings, so you explain things once. If you have several jobs to hand off, give each one its own bot. See [Turn tasks you repeat into a Bot](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/use-cases#turn-tasks-you-repeat-into-a-bot) in the official docs.

## Hand it the goal

Put your "I want …, so that …" sentence in your very first message. For bookings, it could look like this:

<Copy id="goal" title={`Swap in your own "I want …, so that …" sentence`} code="Hi Potato. There's a goal I'd like you to own: I want every new customer's booking scheduled, with none slipping through, so that every customer who reaches out turns into business. This is your job from now on, so please remember it. Then choose a name and a picture for yourself based on this goal." />

Asking it to remember follows the official advice. Give a bot its job in the first message and ask it to remember, because a bot usually decides on its own what to keep. Letting it pick a name and picture based on the goal comes from the author. See [Get started](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/get-started#3-meet-your-first-bot) in the official docs.

If the sentence never comes out right, don't force it. Let the bot interview you:

<Copy id="interview" code="Hi Potato. I'd like you to own a goal, but I'm not sure how to describe it well. Could you ask me questions and interview me until we have a goal I'm happy with?" />

It asks, you answer, and you keep going until the goal sounds right to you.

With the goal handed over, the next step is telling it how you do the work.

## Stuck?

Pick whichever is closest to yours: saving up for a car, a house, or your kids' college; scheduling new customer bookings for your small business; making your customers' experience good enough that they leave good reviews; hiring someone new for your small business; gathering feedback so you can improve your product; preparing slides for a weekly meeting; or building new features for your product.</p>
