A lot of people start a bot off with "check my kid's school emails" or "answer this confirmation email." It'll do that, and only that. In [the article](https://x.com/poteto/status/2123159537016533493), poteto points out a mistake that comes up often, which is treating the process as the goal. Filling out a form or sending an email is just one step in the process. The goal is something you have to dig for.

<mark><strong>Keep asking "what is this for?" until you reach the result you actually care about. That's your goal.</strong></mark>

## Take school notices, and ask twice

Say you're a parent, and the school and your kid's after-school classes send several notices a week. Every evening you go through your inbox, and after a while the job in your head becomes "read the notices." Hand it to a bot that way, and it'll read them for you, and you're still the one who has to remember.

Ask one level up. What's reading them for? So nothing slips. Ask once more. What slips? The parent-teacher meeting you forgot, the fee that's now overdue, the thing your kid was supposed to bring that you only remember on the way out the door. Now it's clear. You want none of that to slip, and reading the notices is just one step.

## For work, ask whether it earns money or saves it

Work goes the same way, and the author gives a very practical place to land. What does this do for the business? Does it earn you money or save you money?

Take a small shop. Each time a new customer gets in touch, you read the email, check your schedule, and send a confirmation. Do that long enough and the job turns into "send confirmation emails." Ask what the email is for, and it's so every new customer's booking is scheduled. Ask again, and a customer with a booking actually shows up, so it earns you money. The other side is saving money, and the author's example is stock. You fill in a stock sheet every week, and it's really for ordering just the right amount, not too much and not too little. On the earning side, the author also mentions closing a sale and building new features.

If you can't work it out yourself, that's normal. For work, ask your manager or a colleague. For home, ask the people you live with. Or just ask the bot.

## Once you know that, the process can change

Why dig this deep? Because a bot that knows where the finish line is can help find the route. Think of dinner. What you want is to eat, and on a tired night ordering in gets you there just as well. If that parent only says "read the notices," the bot reads them. If it knows the goal is "nothing slips," it might find an easier way, like putting every date from a notice on your calendar and reminding you the day before. That's why the author warns against getting too attached to your current process. It's a means to an end.

It's also why this step covers the goal and not the process. The process comes in step 2. If you'd rather the bot follow your process exactly, you can say so then.

## One goal, one bot

Once the goal is clear, give it a bot of its own. Make a new <Term k="bot" /> as in [beginner lesson 2](/en/start/2-first-bot/). Any name will do. If nothing comes to mind, call it Potato and let it pick a name in a minute.

Why a new one? The official docs say a bot with one job builds more useful background than a catch-all helper. A bot that only handles school notices keeps what it learns about them, like which teacher sends what and how often fees come due, so you explain things once. If you have several jobs to hand off, give each one its own bot. See [Turn tasks you repeat into a Bot](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/use-cases#turn-tasks-you-repeat-into-a-bot) in the official docs.

## Hand it the goal

Put the goal you dug out in your very first message. Say what's bugging you first, then what you want. For school notices, it could look like this:

<Copy id="goal" title="Swap in your own job" code="Hi Potato. My kid's school and after-school classes send so many notices that I always miss one or two. Either I forget to pay something, or I find out at breakfast that they were supposed to bring something. From now on this is your job, so remember it: don't let me miss anything those notices ask for. Then pick a name and a picture for yourself based on this job." />

Asking it to remember follows the official advice. Give a bot its job in the first message and ask it to remember, because a bot usually decides on its own what to keep. Letting it pick a name and picture based on the goal comes from the author. See [Get started](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/get-started#3-meet-your-first-bot) in the official docs.

Other jobs work the same way. A student might say, "My deadlines are spread across school emails and the course site, and I always remember right before they're due. That's yours now. Don't let me miss another assignment." A freelancer might say, "I keep forgetting to send the invoice when a job's done, and the money takes forever to come in. Invoicing and chasing payment are yours now. Don't let any job go unpaid."

If nothing you say sounds right, don't force it. Let the bot interview you:

<Copy id="interview" code="Hi Potato. There's something I'd like you to take over, but I can't quite say what I want from it. Could you interview me? One question at a time, until we both think, yes, that's it." />

It asks, you answer, and you keep going until you're nodding.

With the goal handed over, the next step is telling it how you do the work.

## Stuck?

<Q q="I don't know which job to hand over yet.">
<p>The author suggests a few starter goals in the article: saving up for a car, a house, or your kids' college; scheduling new customer bookings for your small business; making your customers' experience good enough that they leave good reviews; hiring someone new for your small business; gathering feedback so you can improve your product; preparing slides for a weekly meeting; or building new features for your product. Everyday ones work too, like which assignments are due this week, your monthly expense report, your parents' checkups and prescriptions, or the job sites you check every day. Pick something you do every few days that bugs you.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="I don't like the name it picked.">
<p>Click the bot's name at the top of the chat to open its details, then click the name again to edit it. Or just ask the bot to pick another one.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="My goal is too big for one bot.">
<p>The month-end close, for example, or taking the family away for a few days, takes several jobs. Ask your <Term k="主 bot" /> to set up a few bots that each own one part. <a href="/en/guide/7-team/">Step 7</a> covers building a team of bots.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="It started working right away.">
<p>Send "Stop now." Your message takes priority over the work in progress, though actions it already finished are not undone. In the next step, the prompt tells it not to start yet.</p>
</Q>
