Potato now handles your bookings, reads your reviews, and starts on its own every morning. You keep handing it more. With that much work, it's time to think about who does what.

Think about how a car repair shop runs. The front desk greets customers and writes down what needs fixing, a mechanic does the repair, and the cashier settles the bill. The car passes from one person to the next, and each stage has one person in charge. You don't chase down a mechanic for everything, either. You tell the front desk, and the front desk hands it to the right person. If two mechanics started on the same car, they'd duplicate the work and talk over each other.

A team of bots works the same way.

<mark><strong>One job per bot, and one owner for each stage of a handoff.</strong></mark>

## Your primary bot is the front desk

Your <Term k="主 bot" />, the one with a star in the sidebar, is your main point of contact. It checks in with you on its own and hands work to your other bots. Give it a request, and it can pass the work to the bot that owns that job, so you don't have to remember which job belongs to whom.

If you don't have a primary bot yet, you'll see **Introducing your primary Bot**. Choose **Create primary Bot** to make one, or **Choose an existing Bot** to pick one. To switch later, right-click your primary bot and choose **Replace with different Bot**.

## When to give a job its own bot

Most bots work best with one clear job. A bot with one job builds up memory for it, so you only explain it once.

So start new work with your primary bot. When the same kind of request keeps coming back, with its own sources, rules or schedule, give it a bot of its own, or ask your primary bot to create one:

<Copy id="newbot" code="The Daily booking check keeps coming to you, and it has its own apps, rules and schedule. Make me a bot that does only this job, name it after the job, and tell it the process and rules we agreed on. Before you create it, tell me how you'll split the work." />

Your bots can also suggest a bot with one job, or create one on their own. To keep your list short, have them ask you before they create one. See the official page on [your primary bot and bots with one job](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/how-grok-bot-works#your-primary-bot-and-bots-with-one-job).

## Split a bigger goal across bots

Some goals take more than one job, like a product launch or the month-end close. For those, ask your primary bot to set up a few bots that each own one part. They pass the work along, and you check the result. The month-end close, for example, could split like this:

<Copy id="team" code="I want the books closed on the 1st of every month, so that I only have to look at the items that don't match. Set up three bots, each owning one part: one matches invoices in the finance inbox to purchase orders, one matches expense reports to receipts, and one lists every mismatch for my sign-off. Tell me how you'll split it and who hands to whom, and create them once I confirm. Don't approve or pay anything." />

Bots hand work to each other in two ways. One is a direct message. One bot messages another, that bot wakes up, handles it and replies later, and you can see the handoff in the conversation. The other is a group chat. Choose **New chat**, add two to six bots in **To:**, and give each stage an owner from the start:

<Copy id="group" code="@Researcher gather the source material and link every claim. @Writer turn the findings into a launch draft. @Reviewer check the draft against the sources and list only blocking issues. Don't publish anything." />

Two bots on the same stage are like two mechanics on one car. They duplicate work and send noisy updates. Keep usage in mind too. Every bot reply counts, including group replies and messages between bots, so fewer bots means fewer replies. And these bots share one computer and one Vault, so separate bots aren't walled off (see [step 4](/en/guide/4-apps/)).

See the official pages on [a team of bots for a bigger goal](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/use-cases#build-a-team-of-bots-for-a-bigger-goal) and [handing work off](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/chat-and-collaboration).

## Share with teammates

A bot that works well is one your teammates may want too. There are two ways to share, and the difference is whether they get their own copy or the same bot you use:

| | Share a template | <Term k="team bot" text="Team Bot" /> |
| --- | --- | --- |
| They get | Their own copy | The same bot as you |
| Apps and keys you added | Remove keys before you share (see below) | Yes |

**Share a template.** Open the bot's details, choose **Share**, then **Create a copy**. The bot posts a template in the chat. Choose **Publish**, then **Copy link**. The other person opens the link and chooses **Add to Grok Bot**. They never get your computer, logins or chat history.

<u>Anyone with a public template link can open it and see the bot's name, description, skills and routines. Remove API keys, internal URLs and customer data before you share.</u>

**Team Bot.** It needs a Team or Enterprise plan and at least one other person on your team. Everyone uses the same bot, with its apps, secrets, skills and files, but each person's chat stays private. In your own chat, it shows an **Allow** card before it uses your apps. See the official page [Team Bots](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/team-bots).

## Dr Eggbot, a bot that designs bots

By now you might think that splitting work across a team of bots means mastering every step first. But the author taught everything they know about automating work to <Term k="Dr Eggbot" />. It's a bot designer, and it creates high-quality bots for you. That's the article's last section, [Install Dr Eggbot](https://x.com/poteto/status/2123159537016533493). The official docs also suggest starting from it to set up several bots at once.

As the official description puts it, Dr Eggbot asks you a few questions, then sets up the bots for you, one job each. Open its [marketplace page](https://x.ai/bot/marketplace/bots/dr-eggbot-v2) and choose **Import Bot** to add your own copy. Then hand it your goal and your process:

<Copy id="eggbot" code="Dr Eggbot, I want to hand this goal to a few bots: <goal>. This is how I usually do it myself: <process>. Ask me questions one at a time first. Then tell me in simple English which bots you'd create, what each one does, and what it doesn't do. Create them once I confirm." />

You started in step 1 with one sentence about a goal. Now you have a team of bots, each with its own job, starting on their own. That's the whole guide. For specific jobs from here, the [how-to guides](/en/howto/) have ready-made recipes.

## Stuck?

<Q q="Several bots are doing the same thing.">
<p>Keep one owner per stage. Tell them: "Don't message other bots. Ask me first." To keep the rule, ask each bot to remember it.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="I have too many bots and the sidebar is a mess.">
<p>Use <strong>Hide from sidebar</strong>. The bot keeps its history and its routines keep running. Deleting is different. It erases the bot's conversations, memory and routines, and can't be undone.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="I don't have a Team plan. Can I give a bot to a teammate?">
<p>Yes, share a template. They get their own copy, without your computer, logins or chat history.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="I want a login to be available to one bot only.">
<p>You can't. All your bots share one computer and one Vault. Sign out of sites you no longer need, and delete sensitive files when the work is done.</p>
</Q>
