Potato has been handling your bookings your way for a while, and it's doing fine. But how would it know what could go better? If it has to wait for you to spot a problem and come back to tell it, it can only improve as fast as you find the time.

The author's approach is to switch places for a moment. If I were doing this job myself and wanted to keep getting better at it, where would I look? You'd probably read customer replies and check your online reviews. So let the bot look there too.

<mark><strong>When your bot can see feedback for itself, it can work out what to improve on its own.</strong></mark>

## One bad review, and it finds out why

The author gives an example. A customer says on Yelp that they waited a long time before anyone helped them. If you saw that review, you'd probably pull up your records for that day and look for what held things up.

The bot can do the same. If it can look up that review on Yelp for itself, and also look up your records for that customer that day, it can see what may have gone wrong.

<Copy id="yelp" code="A customer on Yelp says they waited a long time before anyone helped them. Find that review, then check our booking records for that customer that day. Tell me what probably went wrong and how to avoid it next time. Only look; don't change anything, and don't reply to the review yet." />

For a site with no app, it uses the browser on its <Term k="云端电脑" />, as in step 4. Replying to a review is speaking in public, so have it show you a draft first. For sorting reviews and drafting replies, see [answer reviews and sort customer feedback](/en/howto/reviews-feedback/).

## Give it places where it can see feedback

One bad review is only the start. For the bot to keep spotting problems on its own, give it the apps and tools to get feedback for itself. If it handles your bookings, for example, let it watch your email, social media, and your Google and Yelp reviews. When it sees customer feedback there, it can suggest changes to your process on its own.

You don't have to work out every feedback source alone. Have the bot list them first:

<Copy id="sources" code="Potato, you're handling my bookings now. I want you to see customer feedback for yourself, so that you can spot where we're falling short. If you were me, where would you look? List each place, the app you'd connect or the site you'd open, and what you need from me. Tell me your plan in simple English first, and connect things only after I confirm." />

Once you've confirmed the list, it connects them the way step 4 shows. The idea comes from the article's section [Let your bots improve themselves](https://x.com/poteto/status/2123159537016533493).

## The more you keep on a computer, the more it can help

How good its ideas are depends on how much data it can reach. So, as the author puts it, the more computerized your business, the better for bots and automation.

But a lot of useful data never gets written down. The good news is that Grok Bot can build you a tool to record it. The author's example is a car repair shop that has the bot build a small app for the computer and phone. For each customer visit, it logs when they arrived at the shop, when they spoke to a service rep, when the shop took their keys, and when service started and finished. With those times you can work out how long people waited, then look for why.

<Copy id="logger" code="I run a car repair shop. I want to know how long each customer waits, so that I can find out what holds them up. Build me a small tool I can use on my computer and my phone. For each visit it logs a few times: when they arrived, when they spoke to a service rep, when we took their keys, and when service started and finished. Tell me how you plan to build it first, and start once I confirm." />

If you don't run a repair shop, swap those times for the moments that matter most in your line of work.

## Use the same ID to connect data across apps

Once you have more data, there's another snag. The same customer is a name in your booking system, an email address in your inbox, and just an order number in your repair log. All three are the same person, but to match them up, you need something all three records share.

The author's suggestion is to give the data an identity, an ID. That could be a customer ID or email address, an order number, a part number, a barcode, or an employee ID. When the same customer or order has the same ID in every app you use, the bot can bring the data together and understand what's really going on.

<Copy id="ids" code="I want the same customer to match up across the booking system, email, and the repair log, so that you can bring their records together. First look at how the same customer matches across those three apps today and where they don't match, then suggest one ID we should use everywhere. Suggestions only; don't change any data yet." />

## It also comes to you on its own

Some things your bot will bring to you without being asked. Your <Term k="主 bot" />, the one with a star in the sidebar, doesn't only wait for instructions. When it spots work it can do for you, it offers to take it on. If Gmail signs you out, for example, it tells you in its chat and offers to reconnect.

It always offers first, though. It starts only when you say yes, and it still asks before risky actions, the approvals from step 4. If it messages you too often, turn off that bot's **Notifications**. On the phone, the switch is in the bot's profile. On desktop, ask the bot to turn them off. That stops the alerts, not the check-ins. See the official page [When your bot takes initiative](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/initiative).

Your bot now does the job your way, and it can read feedback and suggest improvements by itself. But it still waits for you to ask before it works. Next, use a <Term k="routine" /> to have it start on its own, for example to check your reviews every week.

## Stuck?

<Q q="Most of my business isn't on a computer, so there's little data.">
<p>Start by logging one thing. Have it build a small tool like the repair shop's, record a few weeks, and then ask it to look.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="The review site needs a login and it can't get in.">
<p>Save the login in Vault, or fill in the secure form when it sends one. Never type the password into chat. See <a href="/en/guide/4-apps/">step 4</a>.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="The same customer's name is spelled differently in different apps.">
<p>Names aren't reliable, so match on an email or customer ID instead. Use the prompt above to have it list the mismatches first.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="It suggested a change I don't want.">
<p>Tell it no, and say why. It won't act on a suggestion you haven't agreed to.</p>
</Q>
