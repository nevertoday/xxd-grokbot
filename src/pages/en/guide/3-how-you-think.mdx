When you explain a process, the easiest things to leave out aren't the steps. They're the parts you know so well that you do them without thinking. The author, poteto, makes a point of this in [the article](https://x.com/poteto/status/2123159537016533493).

<mark><strong>The calls you make from experience, without thinking, are exactly what your bot is missing.</strong></mark>

## The things you do without thinking

Take bookings again. While you book customers, you're keeping track of a lot. Some services take longer and need a bigger slot. Some jobs only certain people can do. Some appointments need extra time between them. To you this is obvious, which is why you probably didn't mention any of it in step 2. The bot doesn't know any of it.

Think back to doing the task yourself. What are you paying attention to? What makes you do something differently? That's what to tell it. The author points out two decisions in particular, below.

## The email says 3 PM and the system says 4 PM. Which wins?

Information often lives in more than one place. A customer's email says 3 PM, and your booking system says 4 PM. You'd know which one to trust. The bot won't, so tell it:

<Copy id="trust" title="Swap in your own rule" code="From now on, when an email and the booking system show different appointment times, trust the booking system and tell me about that booking. Remember this rule." />

For facts that change, like appointment times, have it check the source system each time instead of going from memory. The official docs say the same thing. Memory doesn't replace the real source, so before decisions that matter, ask the bot to reopen the current data. See [Memory is not a source of truth](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/bots#memory-is-not-a-source-of-truth).

## The email went out. Is the booking done?

No. Once the bot sends the confirmation email, it's easy to think the booking is finished. But if the booking never saved, or saved with the wrong time, the customer turns up for a slot that isn't there. The author is clear that a sent email doesn't make a finished booking. The appointment has to be saved correctly and the customer has to get a confirmation, and only when both are true is it done.

So don't take "done" on faith. Each time, ask for something you can check, like a link to the booking record, a screenshot, or a short log of what it did.

<Copy id="done" code="When you finish each booking, tell me what you checked, and include a link to the booking record or a screenshot." />

## Have it keep track of what's still missing

Sometimes a booking stalls halfway. Say the customer hasn't replied with a day that works. Have the bot note that booking and what's missing, so it can pick it back up later. That way it won't forget the booking, and it won't create a second one by accident.

<Copy id="pending" code="If a booking is missing details, keep track of it and note what's missing. Before you pick it back up, check what you tracked, so you don't create a duplicate booking." />

## Test it on real examples

You don't need to explain every case up front, and you couldn't anyway. Pick a few real examples, ideally one where something went wrong or you had to do things differently. Ask the bot how it would handle each one, then tell it what it got right and what it got wrong.

<Copy id="example" title="Swap in your own example" code="Here's a booking from last month: <what happened>. How would you handle it?" />

Even after a few rounds, you've probably left something out. So turn it around and let the bot ask you:

<Copy id="ask-me" code="Potato, help me find what I haven't told you about this process yet. Ask me how I make decisions, where I get my information, how I check the work, and when you should ask me for help. Ask one question at a time. Then walk me through a few examples so I can give you feedback. Don't automate anything yet." />

## Turn each decision into a rule it remembers

Every decision you've explained is a rule it will need every time, so ask it to remember, the way the "trust the booking system" message does. Other rules work the same way, for example:

<Copy id="rule" code="Remember: from now on, never send messages to anyone outside without my approval." />

Details that only apply to one task can just go in that message. They don't need to be remembered. See [Edit a Bot](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/bots#edit-a-bot) in the official docs.

<Callout type="tip" title="Once it works, save it as a skill">
Once it gets the whole task right with you, ask it to save the method as a <Term k="技能" />. The skill holds the steps, the decision rules, what to hand back, and what needs your OK first, and next time the bot follows it. [Step 6](/en/guide/6-routines/) shows how.
</Callout>

By now the bot knows your goal, your process, and how you make your calls. Next, connect the apps it needs.

## Stuck?

<Q q="It made a duplicate booking.">
<p>Tell it to keep track of bookings that are missing details, and to check its notes and the booking system before it creates a new one. Then ask it to remember that rule.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="It's following a rule that's out of date.">
<p>Ask "What do you remember about me?" to find the rule. Then correct it: "That's out of date. Now it's ..."</p>
</Q>
<Q q="There are too many cases to explain.">
<p>You don't need all of them now. Start with a few real examples, then use the prompt above so it asks you about the rest one question at a time.</p>
</Q>
