By now Potato knows what you want, how you usually do it, and how you make the calls. But it still can't reach the things you use every day, like your email, your booking system, or the sites you sign in to. It's like a new helper at your shop who understands the job but hasn't been given the keys yet.

You may hesitate before handing them over. Will it send emails it shouldn't, or spend money? But think about what you'd tell a new helper anyway: handle the everyday stuff yourself, and check with me before you spend money, send anything out, or delete anything. Grok Bot works the same way, so this step does two things.

<mark><strong>Hand it your apps and a computer to work on, and agree on which things it must ask you about first.</strong></mark>

## Have it connect the apps itself

Once the process is where you want it, the author lets the bot start and [connect the apps and tools it needs](https://x.com/poteto/status/2123159537016533493). You don't have to hunt for each one yourself. Just ask:

<Copy id="connect" code="Potato, list the apps our process needs and connect them for me one at a time. When one needs me to sign in, send me the connect card." />

When an app needs you to sign in, the bot shows a **Connect** card in the chat. Open it, sign in in your browser, and finish **Authorize** or **Authenticate**. From your next message on, the bot can use that app.

An app connected this way is a <Term k="连接的应用" />. For services that have one, like Gmail, Notion and Slack, an app is more reliable than having the bot click through the website. It uses the account you signed in with, so it can only do what that account can already do. It can't raise your access or change sharing. And the connection belongs to your account, not to one bot, so every bot you have can use it.

To connect one yourself, open **Connect apps** in the sidebar, find the app, add it and sign in. Connected apps show under **Installed**. To point the bot at a particular app for a task, type `@` in the message box and pick it. See the official page on [connecting an app](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/computer-and-apps#connect-an-app).

## No app for that site? It uses its own computer

Not every site has an app. For those, the bot opens a browser on its <Term k="云端电脑" /> and clicks and types its way through. That computer lives in the cloud, with a browser, a command line and files, so it keeps working after you close your laptop. To watch, click the computer preview on the right of the conversation, and you'll see its clicks, typing and page changes.

The thing to remember is that all the bots on your account share this one computer, a bit like the one computer everyone at the shop takes turns on. When one bot signs in to a site, the others are signed in too. Every bot can see the files on it, and lasting ones belong in `/workspace`. Each bot gets its own screen, so several bots can use a browser at the same time.

<u>If a login or file is meant for one bot only, keep it off this computer.</u> Separate screens and separate bots keep work apart, but they aren't a security boundary. See the official page [Use the computer and apps](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/computer-and-apps).

## On your own computer, it asks every time by default

The cloud computer is separate from the Mac or Windows machine in front of you. A bot runs commands on your machine only if you allow it.

The setting is **Execution on this computer**, under **Settings → Computer → Your computer**, and it has three choices. The default is **Ask every time**. With **Always allow**, it stops asking. The docs recommend **Never allow** unless a bot has a specific reason to work on your local files.

The first time a bot wants to run a command on your machine, the chat asks you. Choosing **Always allow** or **Never** there sets it for every bot, and you can change it later in Settings. See [control access to your local computer](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/approvals-security-and-privacy#control-access-to-your-local-computer).

## Logins go in Vault, never in chat

Think about how you'd give a new helper the password to your booking system. You probably wouldn't type it into a chat. In Grok Bot, the place for these things is <Term k="Vault" />. It holds your logins, cards and contact info, and you manage it in **Settings → Vault**.

To sign in to a site, a bot first looks for a login saved in **Vault**. If there isn't one, it checks a connected password manager such as 1Password, and only if neither has it does it send you a **Secure Form**. Either way, passwords from Vault and values from the form go straight into the page, and the bot never sees them.

Some steps no form can do, like single sign-on, a passkey, a CAPTCHA or a bank card check. Then the bot hands you the computer. Choose **Take over**, do just that one step, choose **I'm done**, and tell it to continue.

<Callout type="warn" title="Cards saved in Vault">
A card saved in Vault fills in at checkout without asking you each time. In chats in the app, a bot can also see a saved card's number and security code. If you want to approve every payment yourself, don't save the card. Leave **Securely save to Bot's vault** unchecked on the form, and remove any card you've already saved in **Settings → Vault**.
</Callout>

<u>Never type a password, one-time code or API key into the chat.</u> For an API key, wait for the bot's secure card. To save or remove items, see [logins and cards go in Vault](/en/howto/vault-logins/). The official page is [Vault and secure sign-in](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/vault).

## What it asks you about first

Back to that new helper. You wouldn't want them asking about every little thing, or you might as well do it yourself. But before they spend money or send something out, you'd like a heads-up.

Grok Bot draws the same line. Before the bot runs a command, uses an app or acts on its computer, an automatic check called Auto Review looks at the action, and it's on by default. Most actions just go ahead. When one needs your OK, the bot shows an <Term k="审批" /> card with the action and its values, then waits. **Allow once** and **Always allow** both do the action now. The difference is what happens later:

| You choose | This time | Similar actions later |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Allow once** | It goes ahead | No rule is saved |
| **Always allow** | It goes ahead | Saved as a rule, so similar actions run on their own |
| **Deny** | It doesn't | It won't try again or ask about it until you send another message |

**If you can't tell what a card would do, don't approve it.** Ask the bot to explain it plainly, or to make a draft first.

It stops and waits for you in these cases:

- **You wrote an "ask first" rule.** Rules live in **Custom Rules** under **Settings → General**, and this kind is **Ask first**. An **Ask first** rule always stops a matching action. When an allow rule (such as one saved by **Always allow**) and an **Ask first** rule both match, **Ask first** wins.
- **It would send mail from its own address, and you didn't ask for that email in chat.** Mail written by a routine, for example, waits as a draft until you press **Send**.
- **It would run a command on your own computer.** By default it asks every time, as in the setting above.

Auto Review counts only your own messages as requests, so text in an email or on a web page doesn't count. But Auto Review is model-based, so don't treat it as your only safeguard. And an approval covers what the bot is about to do. It can't undo work that's already finished.

So the safest approach is to do both. First, set the boundary when you hand over the job:

<Copy id="boundary" code="Remember this rule: before you send a message, publish anything, pay, delete anything, or change permissions, show me what you'll do and the new values, and wait for my OK." />

Then add an **Ask first** rule in **Custom Rules**. Memory alone isn't enough, but an **Ask first** rule always stops a matching action. Keep each rule to one action, like "Ask first before sending any external email." For more examples, see [what it asks you first: approvals and privacy](/en/howto/approvals-privacy/). The official page is [Approvals, security, and privacy](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/approvals-security-and-privacy).

Your bot now has your apps, a computer to work on and a safe place for logins, and it knows what to ask you about first. Next, let it find its own feedback and get better on its own.

## Stuck?

<Q q="It says an app isn't connected.">
<p><strong>Added</strong> only means the app is installed, not that you're signed in. Open it in <strong>Connect apps</strong>. If it shows <strong>Needs auth</strong> or <strong>Disconnected</strong>, choose <strong>Authenticate</strong> and sign in again.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="I closed the sign-in page before finishing.">
<p>Choose <strong>Reconnect</strong> on the app, or <strong>Reopen</strong> on the chat card, and the page comes back. Finish within about 10 minutes, or start again from the card.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="My work Google account says an admin needs to review it.">
<p>If the page says <strong>Your admin needs to review Grok</strong>, your company hasn't approved it yet. Ask your admin to trust Grok in the Google Admin console, then authorize again. Switching browsers or reconnecting won't help. The steps are on the <a href="https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/computer-and-apps#if-google-says-an-admin-needs-to-review-grok">official page</a>.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="A site keeps asking it to sign in again.">
<p>Some sites expire sessions quickly. Ask the bot to pause and tell you, rather than try to get around the site's check.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="I chose Always allow and regret it.">
<p>That rule is in <strong>Custom Rules</strong> under <strong>Settings → General</strong>. On desktop, use the delete button next to the rule.</p>
</Q>
