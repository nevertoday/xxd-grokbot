When people hear "automation," a lot of them think, "So I'd have to write code?" A year or two ago, that was true. You had to know how to build machines or how to write code. In [the article](https://x.com/poteto/status/2123159537016533493), poteto says that with Grok Bot, people who aren't technical can do it too.

<mark><strong>You don't need to write code. Say what you want in everyday words, work out the steps with your bot, and it can take the process from there.</strong></mark>

This full guide is about doing exactly that. It's for people who've finished the beginner lessons or already use Grok Bot, so it skips the click-by-click steps. If you've never used Grok Bot, do [the three beginner lessons](/en/start/1-goal/) first, make your first bot, and come back.

## Automation is a machine that bakes the pie for you

The beginner lessons started with an apple pie. The pie is the goal, and buying ingredients, making the dough, baking it and letting it cool are the process. A flat tire works the same way. A car you can drive again is the goal, and jacking it up, taking the old tire off, putting the new one on and putting everything back are the process. The author uses these two examples to make a bigger point. Automation is really a way of thinking that anyone can learn. You keep the goal in mind and break the work into small steps.

So what does automation add to baking a pie at home? A machine that does the process for you. At home, you make each pie by hand. A bakery that bakes lots of apple pies hands that work to machines. Software engineers writing code are doing the same thing, handing the process to a computer. The hard part used to be that you had to build the machine yourself.

## Why anyone can do it now

Because every bot comes with a computer of its own. Where the job needs code, the bot writes it, and most things you can do on a computer, it can do too. The author asks readers who aren't technical to set their doubts aside for a moment, because computers can do far more than you'd expect. So much of work and life runs on computers now that more and more of it can go to a bot, which frees you up for other things.

That's why this guide goes goal first, process second. Step 1 sets the goal. Steps 2 and 3 teach the bot how you do the work and how you make decisions. Before that, here's what Grok Bot itself looks like.

## A bot comes with a computer

A <Term k="bot" /> is an AI helper that works for you. It has a name, a job, and its own conversation. What sets it apart from an AI that only answers questions is that it has a real computer. That computer lives in the cloud (a <Term k="云端电脑" />) and has a browser, files, and a command line.

So it can browse the web in its own browser and move its own mouse to click through pages. Connect an app like Gmail (a <Term k="连接的应用" />), and it works inside the app directly, which is more reliable than clicking through the website. It can also join a meeting and take notes for you. All of this runs in the cloud, so it keeps going after you close your laptop. See [How Grok Bot works](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/how-grok-bot-works) in the official docs.

## All your bots share that one computer

Your account has one cloud computer, and all your bots work on it. Each bot gets its own screen, so several can work at once, and you can watch any screen live. Files and sign-ins are shared, though, so the bots can hand work off. That means when one bot signs in to a site, your other bots are signed in too.

<Callout type="warn" title="Separate bots are not a wall">
Anything on the computer is available to all your bots. Sign out of a service once no bot needs it, and delete sensitive files when the work is done. See [Do my Bots share one computer?](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/faq#do-my-bots-share-one-computer) in the official FAQ.
</Callout>

## One primary bot, plus bots with one job each

Your <Term k="主 bot" /> is the one you talk to most. It checks in with you on its own and hands work to your other bots, so new work can simply start there.

When the same kind of request keeps coming back with its own sources, rules, or schedule, give it a bot of its own, or ask your primary bot to make one. A bot that has one job remembers what it learns about that job, so you explain things once. Step 1 starts by making a bot like that. See [Your primary Bot and Bots with one job](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/how-grok-bot-works#your-primary-bot-and-bots-with-one-job).

## What a bot remembers

The official docs have a good example. Say you have a bot for travel and expenses, and you tell it, "Sign me up for the same conference as last year and expense it." You never said which conference, which pass, or which card. It knows anyway. Its work log has last year's 2-day pass, and you once gave it a rule to use the work card for conferences.

Each bot has its own memory, and that's what it keeps: how you like to work (short morning briefs, say), the rules you give it ("Ask me before you send an email"), and a dated log of its work. Only a few facts, like your name and time zone, are shared by all your bots. Tell one, and the others know.

A bot decides on its own what to keep, and it doesn't ask first. To make sure it keeps something, say "Remember ...". Your own bots don't have a memory screen, so to see, fix, or remove a memory, just ask in the chat:

<Copy id="memory" title="See, fix, or remove a memory" code={`What do you remember about me?
That's out of date. I moved to Denver.
Forget that I'm on the launch team.`} />

Two things don't belong in memory. Numbers that change, like prices, go stale there, so ask the bot to look them up each time. Logins and cards go in <Term k="Vault" />. See [What a Bot remembers](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/bots#what-a-bot-remembers).

## Where you talk to it

Mostly in the desktop app, which runs on macOS, Windows, and Linux. There's also a phone app for iPhone and Android. Sign in with the same account, and it's the same bots and the same conversations.

There are a few other places too. You can chat with your bots in X Chat, which is still rolling out. Tag @bot in a post on X to hand that post to your bot. And if your team works in Slack or Microsoft Teams, you can add a <Term k="team bot" /> there so your team can reach it.

You don't have to type, either. Dictate a message, or start a voice chat with the bot. See [Where do I talk to Grok Bot?](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/faq#where-do-i-talk-to-grok-bot) in the official FAQ.

## Which plans include it

New customers start on The X Subscription, where Premium, Super, and Ultra include Grok Bot and Plus doesn't. Legacy Cursor plans include it too, on every paid individual plan and self-serve Cursor Teams, and those accounts can also link a personal SuperGrok plan or X Premium+.

You sign in to Grok Bot with a Cursor account. On The X Subscription, Grok Bot shares a weekly usage pool with Grok and Cursor.

Plans change, so check [Grok Bot on The X Subscription](https://docs.x.ai/x-subscription/grok-bot) in the official docs. For more, see the how-to [Plans and how usage works](/en/howto/plans-usage/).

That's the whole page in two ideas. Automation means knowing your goal and handing over the process. Grok Bot is a set of bots that share one cloud computer and each keep their own memory. Next, start with the goal.

## Stuck?

<Q q="The Grok app says I've hit my limit.">
<p>Grok (the chat app) and Grok Bot are two different apps. A paywall or a <strong>Weekly SuperGrok Limit</strong> in the Grok app is about Grok, not Grok Bot.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="Can I keep two bots from seeing each other's files?">
<p>No. The computer belongs to you, not to a bot, and all your bots share it. Treat anything on it as visible to every bot.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="Will it touch my own computer?">
<p>It works on its own cloud computer. It runs a command on your computer only if you allow it, and by default it asks each time. The setting is under <strong>Settings → Computer → Your computer</strong>.</p>
</Q>
