Is the first thing you do every morning opening the chat and telling Potato "check today's bookings"? It does a great job every time, but every time it's waiting for you to say it. On a day you forget, or you're on the road early, nobody does it.

That's what the [article's section on making bots work automatically](https://x.com/poteto/status/2123159537016533493) is about. Without a <Term k="routine" />, a bot works only when you ask. That's fine at the start, but if you want to save yourself the effort, it has to start on its own.

<mark><strong>Once it starts on time, or when something happens, you're no longer the bottleneck.</strong></mark>

The author's question is, when should the bot do the work? When an email arrives, when someone sends a message, or on a regular schedule, like once a week or every other day? Whichever it is, the author says, ask your bot to set it up, and it will.

There's an order to it, though, and the official docs recommend it too. Get the job right once in chat, save it as a skill, and only then set a routine. When it runs by itself, you probably won't be watching.

## Get it right, then save it as a skill

A <Term k="技能" /> is a set of instructions the bot follows next time. Once you've walked it through the job correctly in chat, have it save one:

<Copy id="skill" code="Save the process we just used as a skill called &quot;Daily booking check.&quot; Include when to use it, which apps it needs, the steps in order, how to check the work, and what to hand back to me. Add one rule: always ask me before contacting a customer." />

Saved skills live in one shared library that every bot can use, though any apps or logins a skill needs have to be ready first. To use one later, just ask for it by name. See the official page on [saving a skill](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/skills-routines-and-automations#save-a-skill).

### Show it once

If you see a **Teach a task** button, you don't have to describe every step. Do the job once the way you usually do, and the bot writes the steps up as a skill:

1. Open your one-to-one chat with the bot, then its computer view.
2. Choose **Teach a task** and describe the result you're about to show.
3. Do the job once the way you usually do, then stop the recording.
4. Read the skill it writes, and try it once on an example that's safe to get wrong.

It records up to 10 minutes of what's on screen, with no microphone. <u>Don't show passwords during the demo; sign-ins go through the secure form.</u>

What it records is a draft. You add what one example can't show, like how to decide, what to do when something fails, and which steps need your OK. The button is rolling out gradually. If you don't see it, have the bot write the skill from your instructions and the task you just finished.

## Tell it when to start

With the skill saved, go to the bot that owns the job and tell it when to start, for example:

<Copy id="schedule" code="Every weekday at 8:00 AM, use the Daily booking check skill on today's bookings, and list anything that needs me in this conversation. Don't contact customers. If the booking system is unavailable, tell me what went wrong instead of using old data." />

That message already covers what a routine needs: what time to start, where the data comes from, what to hand back, what not to do, and what to do when the data isn't there. The only thing missing is the time zone, which comes from **Settings**. More on that below.

<Callout type="ok">
Open the bot's details. A new row appears on the **Details** tab, with the routine's name and when it runs.
</Callout>

A new routine doesn't run right away. It waits for its next time, so if you set "every day at 8:00 AM" at 8:05, the first run is tomorrow at 8. To see it work sooner, use **Test**, covered below.

### Start when something happens

Instead of a schedule, it can wait for something to happen. That event is the <Term k="触发条件" />. It can be a Slack message, a GitHub, Linear, Sentry or PagerDuty event, an email to the bot's own address, or a <Term k="webhook" /> call. Say someone asks in Slack to move a booking:

<Copy id="event" code="When a message in #bookings mentions &quot;reschedule,&quot; open it, handle it with the Daily booking check process, and tell me in this conversation what you changed. Never reply in Slack without my approval." />

Keep the condition narrow. "Every new message" runs all the time, spends usage, and makes it more likely to act on things that don't need the bot. And only new activity counts, so messages from before the trigger existed won't start it.

A few more things you'll run into:

- **Triggers connect separately.** They use integrations on your account, apart from the Slack app from step 4, so you may need to connect again.
- **The bot's email address needs claiming.** Say "Get yourself an email address," then approve it. You get one, and you can't rename or delete it.
- **Email filters go by sender only.** Filter by an address or a whole domain, not by subject.
- **Mail from a routine waits for you.** Mail from the bot's own address stays a draft until you press **Send**.
- **The webhook address is in the routine.** Ask the bot to add a webhook trigger, then open the routine on desktop. The **Webhook** section has the URL and key.

See the official page on [starting work from an event](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/skills-routines-and-automations#trigger-work-from-an-event).

## Open the routine and check it

Once it's set, check that what the bot saved matches what you meant. On desktop, click the bot's name at the top of the chat to open its details. Routines are on the **Details** tab. Each row shows the name and when it runs, or **Paused**, and the switch on the row pauses or resumes it.

Open a routine, and **Instruction** says what each run does, **When to run** says the time or event in words, and a routine started by a webhook also shows **Webhook**. If **When to run** isn't what you meant, choose **Edit** and tell the bot what to fix. The time zone is **Timezone** under **Bot** in **Settings**, and **Auto-detect** follows this computer.

On the phone, find **Routines** in the bot's profile. Tap one to see **Active**, **Schedule**, **Next run**, **Instruction** and **Run history**. **Run history** lists recent runs, with the reason for any that failed.

<u>Deleting a routine can't be undone.</u> To stop it for a while, use **Pause**. Deleting a bot also removes its routines.

## Test once, then let it run

On desktop, open the routine and choose **Test**. The button says **Running…** until it finishes, and the result shows up in the bot's chat.

<Callout type="warn" title="A test run does real work">
It really opens websites, changes files and uses connected apps. So test with data that's safe to get wrong, and keep anything that writes behind approval.
</Callout>

When you read the result, ask yourself:

- Did it use current data?
- Is the format right?
- Can you trace each action to a source?
- Did it stop where it should ask you?
- Did it say clearly when something failed?

Some routines have no **Test** button, and they still run on their schedule or trigger. For those, have the bot do the same work once in chat with safe data, then check its first scheduled run.

One more thing. An approval card raised during a routine expires after 30 minutes if nobody answers, and the action isn't done. To finish it, ask the bot to try again.

## Every run uses your usage

Each run spends usage, so the more often a routine runs, the more it spends. The costliest are an hourly schedule, a short interval, or a trigger on a busy Slack channel. Any of them can use a week of usage in a day. A routine that never runs spends nothing, but **Test** does, because it does the real work.

The limits the docs state:

- **Up to 50 routines per bot.**
- **At least 5 minutes between runs.**
- **Hourly caps**: 300 runs for all your routines together, 200 for any one.
- **The 20 most recent run records** are kept for each routine.

If you're away a long time, Grok Bot may ask whether to keep routines running, and pause them if nobody answers. For plans and usage, see [plans and usage](/en/howto/plans-usage/); the limits are on the [official page](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/skills-routines-and-automations).

Now it doesn't wait for you. It starts on time, or when something happens. In the last step, you split a bigger goal across a few bots, each with its own job.

## Stuck?

<Q q="A routine didn't run on time.">
<p>Check in order. It doesn't show <strong>Paused</strong> (on the phone, <strong>Active</strong> is on). <strong>When to run</strong> has the right time and time zone. A new schedule waits for its next time. For Slack, the message is new, in the watched channel, and matches the phrase. For a webhook, the call returned <code>200</code> and used the current key.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="It failed with Automation run rate limit exceeded.">
<p>It went past an hourly cap. Ask the bot to narrow the trigger or widen the schedule. The docs say a limit shared by all users sometimes causes this too, and a fix is coming. These runs don't retry on their own, so choose <strong>Test</strong> to run it again.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="Usage is dropping fast.">
<p>Pause routines you don't need first. Then ask the bot to widen schedules and narrow Slack triggers. Only after that, consider a bigger plan.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="Another bot can't find a skill I saved.">
<p>Open <strong>Connect apps</strong>, select the connected count next to the title, and turn the skill on for this bot under <strong>Private skills</strong>.</p>
</Q>
<Q q="I came back from a break and my routines are paused.">
<p>When you don't answer for a long time, Grok Bot may pause routines. Look through them when you're back, and choose <strong>Resume</strong> on the ones you need.</p>
</Q>
