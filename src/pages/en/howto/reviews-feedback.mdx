You run a phone repair counter, and one day you see a review online: "Waited forty minutes before anyone even looked at me. Never coming back." It stings, but you honestly can't say what went wrong that day. Were you short-staffed, or did the phone before theirs take forever to open up? If you reply, you might make it worse. If you don't, everyone can see it sitting there. If you run a salon, groom pets, or tutor, you've probably had a review like this too.

The author, poteto, describes an idea in the [article](https://x.com/poteto/status/2123159537016533493) that fits here exactly: <mark>if the bot can read that review for itself and also look up your records for that customer that day, it can see what might have gone wrong.</mark>

When you finish this page, your bot will read new reviews every morning at 9:00 AM. It thanks people for good ones, checks that day's records for bad ones, and posts reply drafts in the chat for you to read before anything goes out. You can also have it group the complaints each week and tell you what to fix.

## The goal isn't answering every review

Answering every review is just a means. What you're after is customers happy enough to leave good reviews, and a clear cause for every bad one. It's also one of the goals the author lists in the article. Make a new <Term k="bot" />, call it Potato, and tell it about your counter:

<Copy id="rf-goal" title="First message: the goal" code="I run a phone repair counter. I only remember to check our reviews online every few days, the bad ones put me in a mood, and I feel like I should answer but I'm scared of saying the wrong thing. From now on, you keep an eye on reviews: thank people for the good ones, and for a bad one, find out what actually happened that day, so the same thing doesn't happen again. Make a note: this one's yours to keep." />

To have it name itself after the job, add a line at the end, the same as in [beginner lesson 2](/en/start/2-first-bot/).

## Tell it how you handle reviews

Now tell it how you do this today: where you look, how you reply, and how you find the problems behind complaints. Here's what the counter's owner might say. Swap in your own business:

<Copy id="rf-process" title="Second message: your process" code={`Most days I find a minute to read the new reviews online. Good ones get a quick thanks. With a bad one, I don't rush to reply. I pull up the counter's repair log for that day and work out what happened first. Each week I group the complaints and see which kind comes up most. What I watch hardest is bad reviews, anyone saying they waited too long, and reviews that name someone on staff. Besides the review sites, customers email us too, and I look at posts that mention us on social media. Nothing goes up until I've read it, and every fix you suggest should link the review it came from. If we change something and the reviews pick up, that's how I know it worked.

Don't answer any reviews yet. Plan for doing all of this yourself, and tell me how you'd look into them and reply.`} />

Review sites mostly don't have an app, so it reads them in the browser on its own computer. If customer email is in Gmail, the first time it needs it a **Connect** card shows up in the chat. Open it and sign in. Wherever its plan for checking and replying is off, fix it there and then, and a few rounds will smooth it out.

## Find out what happened that day, then reply

Before you answer a bad review, you think back through that day, and you know what not to say in public. The bot knows none of this, so tell it:

<Copy id="rf-judge" title="Third message: how you decide" code={`Before you answer a bad review, keep these in mind. Look up that customer's repair log for the day before you write anything. Replies start with an apology, then say what we'll change. Don't argue in public about who was right. Refunds, discounts, and promises like that wait for me. A complaint only counts as something to fix once the same kind shows up a second time. If it's a one-off, just make a note.

I probably haven't covered everything that's OK to say in public and what isn't. Ask me, one question after another.`} />

The first rule is what to do with a review like the one at the top of this page: check the records first, then reply. The second keeps promises that cost money with you, so a reply never commits you to anything. The third keeps you from chasing every single complaint. Only the ones that keep coming back are worth changing how you work.

Then test it on the reply that once made a customer angrier. What would it have written? Tell it where it's off. Until its replies sound right, don't let it run on its own. For whatever situations you haven't covered, [the prompt on the prompts page](/en/howto/prompts/#p-gaps) has it ask you, one by one.

If your counter has never written down when people arrive or how long they wait, there are no records for it to check. Have it build you a small tool to log them first. [Full guide step 5](/en/guide/5-improve/) shows how.

## Every morning at 9, it reads them first

Once you're happy with a few batches of replies, let it check every day on its own. Save the process as a <Term k="技能" />, which it follows each time, and give it a <Term k="routine" /> that says when to start:

<Copy id="rf-routine" title="Fourth message: let it start on its own" code={`Write that up as a skill called "Reviews," and set it on a routine: every day at 9:00 AM, use it to read new reviews and post the reply drafts here. Don't post a single reply online yourself. If a site won't open, tell me which one. Don't just quietly skip it.`} />

After the first few runs, count the new reviews on the site yourself and check them against its list. Each draft for a bad review should note what it found for that day. Last, look at the site, which should have no replies from it.

Set it up after 9:00 AM, and its first run is the next morning. To try it now, open it and choose **Test**. A test really goes and reads the sites, and the replies still only show up in the chat. See [Create a routine](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/skills-routines-and-automations#create-a-routine) in the official docs.

For a weekly summary, set up a second routine the same way, like three suggested changes every Monday morning.

The official docs suggest keeping publishing behind an approval. For what it stops to ask you about, see [Approvals and privacy](/en/howto/approvals-privacy/).

When it can see both the review and that day's records, you can trace a bad review back to the step where the customer was kept waiting.

## Stuck?

<Q q="It can't get into the review site's business account.">
Save the login in <Term k="Vault" /> first, and the bot fills it in without seeing the password. If it still can't get in, like when a QR code is needed, it hands you the computer. Choose **Take over**, finish that step, and choose **I'm done**. Then tell it to continue.
</Q>
<Q q="The suggested changes are too vague.">
Ask it to link each suggestion to the original review and that day's records. Then ask it to keep three things apart: facts from the records, its guesses, and what it couldn't check.
</Q>
<Q q="I want to know if people mention us elsewhere online.">
It can search and read public posts on X without connecting an X account. The official marketplace also has [Overheard](https://x.ai/bot/marketplace/bots/overheard), which watches Reddit, Hacker News, news sites, and X for mentions of you. When something is worth reading, it sends a short weekday digest. It never posts for you.
</Q>
