A customer writes on Yelp that they waited a long time before anyone helped them. You see it, but you can't say what actually went wrong that day. The author, poteto, uses this example in the [article](https://x.com/poteto/status/2123159537016533493), and the author's view is that <mark>if the bot can read that review for itself and also look up your records for that customer that day, it can see what might have gone wrong.</mark>

When you finish this page, your bot will read new reviews every morning at 9:00 AM. It thanks people for good ones, checks that day's records for bad ones, and posts reply drafts in the chat for you to read before anything goes out. You can also have it group the complaints each week and tell you what to fix.

It takes four steps: give it the goal, explain how you do it now, teach it how you decide, then let it start on its own.

## The goal isn't answering every review

Answering every review is only one step in the process. What you actually want is customers happy enough to leave good reviews, and a clear cause for every bad one. It's also one of the goals the author lists in the article. Make a new <Term k="bot" />, call it Potato, and give it that goal:

<Copy id="rf-goal" title="First message: the goal" code="Hi Potato. There's a goal I'd like you to own. I want customers to be happy enough to leave us good reviews, and for every bad review I want to know what went wrong, so that the same problem doesn't happen again. This is your job from now on, so please remember it. Then, based on the goal, choose a name and a picture for yourself." />

## Tell it how you handle reviews

Now tell it how you do this today: where you look, how you reply, and how you find the problems behind complaints. Here's an example. Swap in your own shop's details:

<Copy id="rf-process" title="Second message: your process" code={`Potato, this is how I usually do this myself. Every day I read new reviews. I thank people for good ones, and for bad ones I find out what happened before I reply. Each week I group the complaints and see which kind comes up most. I watch especially for bad reviews, complaints about waiting, and reviews that name a staff member. I use Gmail. For review sites like Google and Yelp, use your own browser. The information comes from review sites, customer emails, social media, and the shop's records for that day. It's right when I've read every reply before it goes out and every suggested change links to the original review. Whether new reviews get better after we change something is our feedback.

Think about how you could take this over from me completely. Before you begin, restate my goal and process in simple words, so I can give you feedback and confirm it. Don't start doing the work yet. Let's get the process planned first.`} />

If typing is slow, press `Cmd/Ctrl+D` and just talk, saying things as they come to you. It restates what it heard first. Correct whatever's off and go a few rounds until it's right. When you're happy, tell it to go ahead. If it needs an app, it shows a **Connect** card in the chat. Open it and sign in.

## Find out what happened that day, then reply

Before you answer a bad review, you think back through that day, and you know what not to say in public. The bot knows none of this, so tell it:

<Copy id="rf-judge" title="Third message: how you decide" code={`Potato, here are a few rules I use to decide. For a bad review, look up that customer's records for the day before you write a reply. Replies start with an apology, then say what we'll change. Don't argue in public. Refunds, discounts, and other promises wait for me. A complaint counts as a problem to fix once the same kind shows up a second time. Note one-offs and wait.

Help me find what I haven't told you about this process yet. Ask me how I make decisions, where I get my information, how I check the work, and when you should ask me for help. Ask one question at a time. Then walk me through a few examples so I can give you feedback. Don't automate anything yet.`} />

The first rule is the Yelp example from the top of this page: check the records first, then reply. The second keeps promises that cost money with you, so a reply never commits you to anything. The third keeps you from chasing every single complaint. Only the ones that keep coming back are worth changing how you work.

When it's done asking, try it on a real case or two that went wrong, like the reply that made a customer angrier. See how it would handle them, then tell it what to fix.

<Callout type="tip" title="Not enough data? Have it build a log">
The article also gives the example of a car repair shop. You can ask the bot to build a small app for your computer and phone that logs when a customer arrives, when they talk to a service rep, when you take their keys, and when service starts and finishes. With those times you can work out how long people waited and where. Link records across apps with one shared ID, like a customer email or an order number.
</Callout>

## Every morning at 9, it reads them first

Once this has gone well a few times, you can let it start on its own. First have it save the process as a <Term k="技能" />, written instructions it follows every time, then set up a <Term k="routine" /> that tells it when to start:

<Copy id="rf-routine" title="Fourth message: let it start on its own" code={`Save the process we just used as a skill called "Reviews." Then set up a routine: every day at 9:00 AM, use that skill to read new reviews and post the reply drafts in this chat. Don't publish any reply online yourself. If a site won't open, tell me which one. Don't skip it quietly.`} />

After the first few runs, count the new reviews on the site yourself and check them against its list. Each draft for a bad review should note what it found for that day. Last, look at the site, which should have no replies from it.

A new routine waits for its next scheduled time. If you'd rather not wait, open it and choose **Test**, but keep in mind that a test does real work. See [Create a routine](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/skills-routines-and-automations#create-a-routine) in the official docs.

For a weekly summary, set up a second routine the same way, like three suggested changes every Monday morning.

The official docs suggest keeping publishing behind an approval. For what it stops to ask you about, see [Approvals and privacy](/en/howto/approvals-privacy/).

When it can see both the review and that day's records, a bad review stops being just a complaint and becomes something you can trace.

## Stuck?

<Q q="It can't get into the review site's business account.">
Save the login in <Term k="Vault" /> first, and the bot fills it in without seeing the password. If it still can't get in, like when a QR code is needed, it hands you the computer. Choose **Take over**, finish that step, and choose **I'm done**. Then tell it to continue.
</Q>
<Q q="The suggested changes are too vague.">
Ask it to link each suggestion to the original review and that day's records. Then ask it to keep three things apart: facts from the records, its guesses, and what it couldn't check.
</Q>
<Q q="I want to know if people mention us elsewhere online.">
It can search and read public posts on X without connecting an X account. The official marketplace also has [Overheard](https://x.ai/bot/marketplace/bots/overheard), which watches Reddit, Hacker News, news sites, and X for mentions of you. When something is worth reading, it sends a short weekday digest. It never posts for you.
</Q>
