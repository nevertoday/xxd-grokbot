Picture a small salon with two stylists. One customer emails to ask if you're free Saturday, another picks a time on your booking page, and someone who already booked emails to push it back. You answer messages between haircuts, and when you go over it all that night, you've got two people at 3 PM on Saturday. Tutors and pet groomers know this too: checking for an open slot, creating the booking, and sending a confirmation, over and over through the day. When you finish this page, your bot will handle new booking requests every 2 hours during opening hours: it finds a slot, creates the booking in your system, and drafts the customer's confirmation for you to send.

The author, poteto, uses booking customers as the running example in the [article](https://x.com/poteto/status/2123159537016533493). The rules below come from it, and the salon is the example we've swapped in. The author's sharpest point is about when a booking counts. <mark>An email alone isn't a booking. It counts once it's saved in the system and the customer has a confirmation.</mark>

It takes four steps: give it the goal, explain how you do it now, teach it how you decide, then let it start on its own.

## The goal isn't a reply, it's a booked customer

Replying to an email is only one step in the process. What you actually want is that every customer who wants to come in gets a good time soon, and no request slips. Make a new <Term k="bot" />, call it Potato, and tell it where you're at and what you want:

<Copy id="bk-goal" title="First message: the goal" code="Hi Potato. I run a small hair salon with two stylists. Some customers book by email, some pick a time on our booking page, and some book and then want to change it. I answer between haircuts, and last week I booked two people into the same slot. I'd like you to take this over: everyone who wants to come in gets a good time soon and a confirmation. That way we don't lose any business and we stop double-booking. This is your job from now on, so remember it. Then pick a name and a picture for yourself that fit the job." />

## Tell it how you book an appointment

Now tell it how you take a booking today: what you check first, where you create it, and how you reply. Here's an example. Swap in your own shop's details:

<Copy id="bk-process" title="Second message: your process" code={`Potato, here's how I do it now. When a booking request comes in, I check who's free that day, create the booking in our booking system, then email the customer to confirm. If a customer changes their time, I edit the booking they already have. Don't make a new one. I have to keep in mind how long each job takes: a haircut is half an hour, color is two hours, only I do perms, and we leave 10 minutes between appointments to clean up. I use Gmail, Google Calendar, and our booking system (the address is ...), and the information comes from customer emails and the shop calendar. It's right when the booking is in the system and the customer got a confirmation. Customer reviews online and our no-show records tell us what we got wrong.

Think about how you could take this off my plate completely. Before you start, tell me back in plain words what you think the goal is and how you'd go about it, and I'll tell you if you've got it. Don't do anything yet. Let's get the plan right first.`} />

If you tutor, swap "how long each job takes" for how long a lesson is and which tutor teaches which subject. If you groom pets, it's how long big dogs and small dogs take and which groomer handles which kind of coat.

If typing is slow, press `Cmd/Ctrl+D` and just talk, saying things as they come to you. It restates what it heard first. Correct whatever's off and go a few rounds until it's right. When you're happy, tell it to go ahead. If it needs an app, it shows a **Connect** card in the chat. Open it and sign in.

## When the email and the system disagree

The author points out that the easiest thing to forget when you explain a process is the calls you make without thinking. The article gives three booking situations, and they make three good rules:

<Copy id="bk-judge" title="Third message: how you decide" code={`Potato, here are a few rules I go by. If the time in a customer's email doesn't match the booking system, the booking system wins, and then you email the customer to double-check. Sending an email doesn't finish a booking. It's done when the booking is saved in the system and the customer has a confirmation. If we're still waiting to hear back from a customer, like they didn't say whether it's a cut or color, keep a note and pick it back up later. Don't create a second booking.

Also, help me figure out what I haven't told you yet: how I make calls, where my info comes from, how I know it's done right, and when you should come to me. One question at a time. Then walk me through a few examples and I'll tell you what's off. Don't set anything to run on its own yet.`} />

The first rule tells it which one to trust when the two disagree. The second is the line from the top of this page, which puts "booked" at the point where both the system and the customer have it. The third guards against booking twice. When a customer is slow to reply and the bot comes back to the request, a glance at its notes shows the booking is already there.

The author also says you don't have to cover every situation up front. Take a few real examples, including ones that went wrong, like the customer who moved their time twice and ended up with both times in the system. Ask how it would handle them, then give it feedback.

## Every 2 hours during opening hours

Once this has gone well a few times, you can let it start on its own. First have it save the process as a <Term k="技能" />, written instructions it follows every time, then set up a <Term k="routine" /> that tells it when to start:

<Copy id="bk-routine" title="Fourth message: let it start on its own" code={`Save what we just worked out as a skill called "New bookings." Then set up a routine: during opening hours, every 2 hours, use that skill to handle new booking requests. Write customer confirmations as drafts and wait for me to send them. If the booking system won't open, tell me in this chat. Don't book from old data.`} />

After the first few runs, open the routine and check that **When to run** says the time you meant. Each confirmation should stop at a draft card that waits for you to choose **Send**. Then look through the booking system, where each customer should have just one booking.

A new routine waits for its next scheduled time. If you'd rather not wait, open it and choose **Test**. A test does real work, so use a fake booking request. See [Create a routine](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/skills-routines-and-automations#create-a-routine) in the official docs.

The more often it runs, the more usage it spends. If every 2 hours is enough, don't make it tighter.

Anything that goes to a customer or takes money, write into the prompt as a stop. For what it stops to ask you about, see [Approvals and privacy](/en/howto/approvals-privacy/).

Once "booked" has a clear meaning, it won't mistake one email for a finished booking.

## Stuck?

<Q q="One customer got booked twice.">
Add a rule: before creating a booking, look the customer up in the booking system by email or phone. The article says to use one shared ID across your apps, like an email address or an order number, so the bot can connect the records.
</Q>
<Q q="Confirmation emails never went out.">
Emails it writes are drafts by default, and they go out only when you choose **Send**. Also, an <Term k="审批" /> card raised by a routine expires after about 30 minutes, and then the action doesn't happen. Tell it in chat to turn its notifications on, so you see these in time.
</Q>
<Q q="The booking system wants a login or a verification code.">
Save the login in <Term k="Vault" />, and the bot fills it in without seeing the password. For a code, a QR scan, or anything else it can't do, it hands you the computer. Choose **Take over**, finish that step, choose **I'm done**, then tell it to continue. Never paste a password into the chat.
</Q>
