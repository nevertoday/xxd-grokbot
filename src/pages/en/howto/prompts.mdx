Want to hand a bot a job but not sure how to open? This page lines up prompts you can send as they are, in the order of poteto's article [Automate Anything with Grok Bot](https://x.com/poteto/status/2123159537016533493): start with the goal, explain the process, teach it how you think, let it improve itself, then make it run on its own. The habit that matters most along the way is to <mark>have it restate first, and let it start only after you confirm.</mark>

The five marked "the author's" are prompts the author wrote in the article (the goal, the interview, the process, the review and the gaps), lightly reworded here. The ones marked "ours" are ideas the article describes without giving any wording, so we wrote them from what it describes. Replace what's in angle brackets with your own details, and Potato with your bot's name.

## Start with the goal

Send this first, right after you make a new bot. Describe the result you want at the end, since filling in a form or sending an email is only a step in the process. The article just has you fill in the goal. We split it into "I want … so that …" so you also say what it's for, which the author suggests thinking about first: does it help you make more money, or spend less?

<Copy id="p-goal" title="Give it the goal and let it name itself (the author's)" code={`Hi Potato, this is the goal I want you to be responsible for: I want <the result you want at the end>, so that <what it's for>. Pick out a name and picture for yourself based on it.`} />

When you only have a rough direction in mind and can't put the goal into words, let it ask you:

<Copy id="p-interview" title="Can't describe the goal? Let it interview you (the author's)" code="Hi Potato, I'd like you to own a goal, but I'm not sure how to describe it yet. Can you ask me some questions and keep interviewing me until we land on a goal I'm happy with?" />

## The process

With the goal set, tell it how you do the job today. Voice dictation works best: talk for a while without organizing your thoughts first. In `<your process>`, cover the steps you take, what you watch out for, and the apps and tools you use. Add how you check the work, where your information comes from, and where feedback comes from.

<Copy id="p-process" title="Tell it how you do it, have it restate, and hold off (the author's)" code={`Potato, here's how I usually do this myself: <your process>

I want you to think about how you could take this over from me completely. Before you begin, restate my goal and process in plain words so I can give you feedback and confirm it. Don't start doing the work yet. Let's get the process planned first.`} />

The first pass doesn't need to be right. Read how it restates things, correct it and fill in what's missing, and go a few rounds. Once you're happy, send this to get it started:

<Copy id="p-proceed" title="Summary right? Tell it to start (ours)" code="Good, go with this process. Start by connecting the apps and tools you need for the job, then go ahead." />

If you'd like it to look for a better or simpler way, have it check the process against the goal. Your current process is only a means to an end, after all, so don't hold on to it too tightly:

<Copy id="p-review" title="Check the process against the goal (the author's)" code="Review our process against my original goal, and if you see a way to make it better or simpler, let me know." />

## Teach it how you think

You do a lot without thinking about it. The author's example is booking customers: you know which services take longer, who can do each job, and when to leave extra time between appointments. That's obvious to you, but your bot has to be told. After you've explained the process, and before it starts for real, have it ask you about these one by one:

<Copy id="p-gaps" title="Have it find what you left out (the author's)" code="Potato, help me find what I haven't told you about this process yet. Ask me how I make decisions, where my information comes from, how I check the work, and when you should come to me. Ask one question at a time. Then walk me through a few examples so I can give you feedback before we automate it." />

You don't need to cover every case up front, either. Pick a few real examples, especially the ones that went wrong or needed special handling, and ask what it would do:

<Copy id="p-examples" title="Test it on real examples (ours)" code={`Here are a few real examples, including some that went wrong: <examples>. How would you handle each one? Walk me through it and I'll give you feedback.`} />

## Let it improve itself

Once it's doing the job, it's best to let it get feedback for itself. The author gives an example: a customer says on Yelp that they waited a long time before anyone helped them. If the bot can look up that review on its own, and pull up that day's records, it can see what went wrong.

<Copy id="p-feedback" title="Have it look for feedback itself (ours)" code={`From now on, watch <reviews, inbox or social media> for customer feedback. When you see a problem, check that day's records to see what went wrong, then suggest how to improve the process.`} />

That's why the more data it has, the better it can see where things go wrong. If the data isn't there, have it build you a small tool to log it. The author's example is a car repair shop: log when customers arrived, when they spoke to someone, when you took their keys, and when work started and finished, and you can work out how long they waited and why. Swap in the moments that matter in your business:

<Copy id="p-log-app" title="Have it build a small tool to log data (ours)" code="Build me a small app I can use on my computer and phone to log each customer visit: when they arrived, when they spoke to someone, when we took their keys, when work started and when it finished." />

When your data is spread across several apps, you also need one ID to tie it together, like an email or order number. For why this matters, see [Consistent IDs](/en/wiki/ids/).

<Copy id="p-ids" title="Tie data together with one ID (ours)" code={`I keep customer records in <app A> and <app B>. Check whether the two can be matched with one ID, like an email or order number, and list anything that doesn't match.`} />

## Make it run on its own

Without a <Term k="routine" />, it only works when you ask. With one, you're no longer the bottleneck. Once a job runs reliably, have it start on a schedule:

<Copy id="p-schedule" title="Start on a schedule (ours)" code="From now on, do this automatically every Monday at 9:00 AM without waiting for me. Once it's set up, tell me when it will run and in which time zone." />

If the work starts with an email or a message, have it wait for that to happen:

<Copy id="p-trigger" title="Start when something happens (ours)" code={`From now on, whenever <a message like this> arrives in <this inbox or Slack channel>, start this job. Keep the trigger narrow, not every message.`} />

## Install Dr Eggbot

Everything the author knows about automating work went into Dr Eggbot, a bot whose job is designing bots. First add it to your Grok Bot from [Dr Eggbot's page](https://x.ai/bot/marketplace/bots/dr-eggbot-v2), then send it this:

<Copy id="p-eggbot" title="Have it design a team of bots (ours)" code={`I want to automate <this job or my business>, so that <the result you want most>. Ask me a few questions first, then design some bots for me, one job each.`} />

## Stuck?

<Q q="It started working before I confirmed.">
Send "Stop now." Then ask it to restate the goal and process and wait for you. When you hand over new work, end the prompt with "Don't start yet."
</Q>
<Q q="Its summary is wrong.">
That's normal. Point out what's wrong, add what it's missing, and ask it to restate again. A few rounds usually does it.
</Q>
<Q q="Can I send these prompts in another language?">
Yes. Bots reply in the language you write to them in.
</Q>
<Q q="I can't think of a name for it.">
Then call it Potato. Once the goal is set, use the first prompt to have it name itself.
</Q>
