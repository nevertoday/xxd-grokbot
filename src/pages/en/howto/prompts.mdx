Want to hand a bot a job but not sure how to open? This page lines up prompts you can send as they are, in the order of poteto's article [Automate Anything with Grok Bot](https://x.com/poteto/status/2123159537016533493): start with the goal, explain the process, teach it how you think, let it improve itself, then make it run on its own. Only one habit really matters along the way: <mark>have it restate first, and let it start only after you confirm.</mark>

The five marked "adapted from the article" come from prompts the author wrote in the article (the goal, the interview, the process, the review and the gaps), put into everyday words. The goal and the interview also open with a line about your situation, which the article doesn't have. The ones marked "ours" are ideas the article describes without giving any wording, so we wrote them from what it describes. Replace what's in angle brackets with your own situation, and Potato with your bot's name.

They're all written the way you'd text someone: your situation first, then what you want. The examples below are how different people would say it, and yours doesn't need to match the template word for word.

## Start with the goal

Send this first, right after you make a new bot. Describe the result you want at the end. Think of dinner: what you want is dinner on the table, and shopping and chopping are just the process. Filling in a form or sending an email is the same, only a step in the process. The article just has you fill in the goal. We added a line about your situation up front, because once the bot hears what's bugging you, it knows why you're asking. The author also suggests thinking first about whether this helps you make more money or spend less.

<Copy id="p-goal" title="Give it the goal and let it name itself (adapted from the article)" code={`Hi Potato. <What's driving you crazy about this right now>. I'd like you to take this over: <the result you want at the end>. That way <what it gets you>. This is your job from now on, so remember it. Then pick a name and a picture for yourself that fit the job.`} />

Here's roughly how different people might say it:

- A student: "My due dates are all over school email and three class sites, and I keep finding out the night before. Just tell me every morning what's due this week, so I stop pulling all-nighters."
- Someone with an office job: "Every month I dig through my camera roll for receipts and check the amounts one by one for my expenses, and it eats a whole evening. Log each receipt as it comes in, and at the end of the month fill in the expense form for me to look over."
- Someone caring for their parents: "I'm the only one keeping track of my parents' checkups, prescriptions, and physicals, and the paperwork is all over the place. Keep an eye on the dates and remind me two days ahead. Put the test results in one table too, so I can just grab it on the way to the doctor."
- A freelancer: "I finish a job and forget to ask the client for the money, and it takes a month or two to come in. When a job's done, draft the invoice and the payment request email for me to check, and tell me if it isn't paid by the due date."
- A content creator: "Every post gets hundreds of comments, my email's full of collab offers, and I've missed brand deals buried in there. Each evening, pull out the collab offers and the real questions, draft replies, and I'll send them."
- A small shop owner: "Customers book by email and on our booking page, and I mix them up. I've double-booked once already. You handle the bookings from now on, and check with me when there's a clash, so nobody comes in for nothing."

When you only have a rough direction in mind and can't put the goal into words, let it ask you:

<Copy id="p-interview" title="Can't describe the goal? Let it interview you (adapted from the article)" code="Hi Potato. There's something I'd like you to take over, but I can't quite say what I want. I just know I'm getting chased around by a pile of little things every day. Can you ask me some questions and interview me until we land on a goal I'm happy with?" />

## The process

With the goal set, tell it how you do the job today. Voice dictation works best: talk for a while without organizing your thoughts first. In `<your process>`, cover the steps you take, what you watch out for, and the apps and tools you use. Add how you check the work, where your information comes from, and where feedback comes from.

You don't need to go down that list item by item. Just talk the way you would to a person. A teacher who writes to parents every week might say: "Friday after school, I export the week's homework records from the school system, pick out the kids who haven't turned things in, and write each of their parents an email saying what's missing and when it's due. My big worry is putting another kid's details in the wrong email. When a parent writes back that their kid did hand it in, I know the system didn't record it."

<Copy id="p-process" title="Tell it how you do it, have it restate, and hold off (adapted from the article)" code={`Potato, here's how I do this now: <your process>

Think about how you could take this over from me completely. Before you begin, play back to me in plain words what you think my goal and my process are, so I can correct you and confirm. Hold off on the actual work, and let's get the process planned first.`} />

The first pass doesn't need to be right. Read how it restates things, correct it and fill in what's missing, and go a few rounds. Once you're happy, send this to get it started:

<Copy id="p-proceed" title="Summary right? Tell it to start (ours)" code="Yep, that's it. Go ahead and connect the apps you need for this, then get started." />

If you'd like it to look for a better or simpler way, have it check the process against the goal. Your current process is only a means to an end, after all, so don't hold on to it too tightly. On a tired night you order in, and dinner still ends up on the table:

<Copy id="p-review" title="Check the process against the goal (adapted from the article)" code="Go back to the goal I started with and look over how we're doing this. If there's a better or simpler way, just tell me. I'm not attached to doing it the old way." />

## Teach it how you think

You do a lot without thinking about it. The author's example is booking customers: you know which services take longer, who can do each job, and when to leave extra time between appointments. If you run a weekend sports team, it's the same: you know whose sign-up needs a second check and who never needs chasing. That's obvious to you, but your bot has to be told. After you've explained the process, and before it starts for real, have it ask you about these one by one:

<Copy id="p-gaps" title="Have it find what you left out (adapted from the article)" code="Potato, help me figure out what I haven't told you yet: how I make calls, where my info comes from, how I know it's done right, and when you should come to me. Ask one question at a time. Then walk me through a few examples and I'll tell you what's off, and after that we'll set it to run on its own." />

You don't need to cover every case up front, either. Pick a few real examples, especially the ones that went wrong or needed special handling, and ask what it would do. Think of the interview you nearly missed, the booking that got moved twice, or the checkup that landed on the same afternoon as something else:

<Copy id="p-examples" title="Test it on real examples (ours)" code={`Let me tell you about a few times this really happened, and a couple of them went badly: <examples>. If it were you, what would you do each time? Talk me through it and I'll tell you if you're right.`} />

## Let it improve itself

Once it's doing the job, it's best to let it get feedback for itself. The author gives an example: a customer leaves a review online saying they waited a long time before anyone helped them. If the bot can look up that review on its own, and pull up that day's records, it can see what went wrong. Feedback can be somewhere else too: for a freelancer it's in client replies, for a content creator it's in the comments, and for a teacher it's in parents' replies.

<Copy id="p-feedback" title="Have it look for feedback itself (ours)" code={`From now on, keep an eye on <where feedback shows up, like reviews online or client replies> yourself. When someone's unhappy, go through that day's records and find the step where it went wrong, then tell me what to change next time. Don't reply to anyone, and don't change anything yet.`} />

That's why the more data it has, the better it can see where things go wrong. If the data isn't there, have it build you a small tool to log it, which is the author's idea too. The author's example is a shop logging the times of each step of a customer's visit. You don't need a shop to use it. If you look after your parents, say, you could have it build a blood pressure log:

<Copy id="p-log-app" title="Have it build a small tool to log data (ours)" code="My dad checks his blood pressure every morning and evening and writes the numbers in a little notebook, and whenever the doctor asks, I can never find them all. Make me a simple tool for my phone. Each time, it logs the date, morning or evening, the top and bottom numbers, and whether he took his pills. At the end of the month, give me a table to bring to his checkup. Just record things, and don't tell me whether the numbers are high. Tell me how you plan to do it first, and start once I say OK." />

Swap in the numbers that matter for your own job.

When your data is spread across several apps, you also need one ID to tie it together, like an email or order number. For why this matters, see [Consistent IDs](/en/wiki/ids/).

<Copy id="p-ids" title="Tie data together with one ID (ours)" code={`I keep customer records in <app A> and in <app B>, and the same person never quite matches up between them. See if there's one thing we can match them on, like an email or an order number, and show me a list of what doesn't match. Don't change anything yet.`} />

## Make it run on its own

Without a <Term k="routine" />, it only works when you ask. With one, you're no longer the bottleneck. Once a job runs reliably, have it start on a schedule:

<Copy id="p-schedule" title="Start on a schedule (ours)" code="From now on, start this yourself every Monday at 9:00 AM. You don't need to wait for me. Once it's set up, tell me when it'll run and in which time zone." />

Hunting for a flat, say, where the good ones go in hours, you could have it check twice a day:

<Copy id="p-rent" title="Flat hunting: check the rental sites twice a day (ours)" code="I'm hunting for a flat and the good ones go in hours. Every day at 8 AM and 7 PM, check the two rental sites I use for two bedrooms, ten minutes' walk to a station, and rent under ... a month. Send me links to new ones only, nothing you've sent before. Don't contact any landlords. When it's set up, tell me what times it runs and in which time zone." />

Twice a day is plenty. The more often it checks, the more usage it spends.

If the work starts with an email or a message, have it wait for that to happen:

<Copy id="p-trigger" title="Start when something happens (ours)" code={`From now on, whenever <a message like this> shows up in <your bot's own email address, or this Slack channel>, start on this. Keep the trigger narrow. Don't jump on every message.`} />

One catch: the email that starts it has to arrive at the bot's own address. A notice from school or a customer emailing to move a booking lands in your own inbox, and mail there doesn't start a routine. To have mail like that start the work, forward it to the bot's address, the way the routine on [Plan a trip](/en/howto/travel/) waits for mail to the bot. Or check on a schedule instead, like the 8 PM routine in [Hand off the family admin](/en/howto/family-admin/).

## Install Dr Eggbot

Everything the author knows about automating work went into Dr Eggbot, a bot whose job is designing bots. First add it to your Grok Bot from [Dr Eggbot's page](https://x.ai/bot/marketplace/bots/dr-eggbot-v2), then send it this:

<Copy id="p-eggbot" title="Have it design a team of bots (ours)" code={`I'd like to hand off <this job or my business> completely. Right now <what's driving you crazy about it>, and what I want most is <the result you want most>. Ask me a few questions first, then design some bots for me, one job each.`} />

Someone who runs a hair salon might fill it in like this: "I'd like to hand off all the busywork at my salon. Right now I'm doing bookings, review replies, and hiring all by myself, and what I want most is to just cut hair."

## Stuck?

<Q q="It started working before I confirmed.">
Send "Hold on, stop for a sec." Then ask it to restate the goal and process and wait for you. When you hand over new work, end the prompt with "Don't start yet."
</Q>
<Q q="Its summary is wrong.">
That's normal. Point out what's wrong, add what it's missing, and ask it to restate again. A few rounds usually does it.
</Q>
<Q q="Can I send these prompts in another language?">
Yes. Bots reply in the language you write to them in.
</Q>
<Q q="I can't think of a name for it.">
Then call it Potato. Once the goal is set, use the first prompt to have it name itself.
</Q>
